## Why this letter was written

*beforeword’s explanation for readers; separate from the sent letter.*

**Starting point.** The starting point is Clark’s statement, quoted in the letter, about thoughts becoming determinate and public objects. The letter focuses on one specific move: describing a revision to a written argument as a correction of thought.

**Why it matters.** Readers need to distinguish a change in wording, which can be compared with an earlier version, from the interpretation of that revision as a change in thought.

**What the letter asks.** The letter asks for one worked example showing what warrants calling the revision a correction of thought. The explanation and its chosen criterion are then open to the same examination as the original text.

**Subject: Revising a sentence, correcting a thought**

---

Dear Professor Clark,

In “Magic Words: How Language Augments Human Computation”, you write: “Our thoughts become determinate and public objects”.

Your account gives linguistic formulations an active role in reshaping cognition.

Please indicate, using one example of revising a written argument, what would warrant describing the revision as a correction of a thought.

beforeword proposes keeping three elements separately inspectable: the written revision, its interpretation as correcting a thought, and the grounds offered for that interpretation.

The terms “thought” and “correction” remain part of the writing under examination. Descriptions of cognitive processes, any proposed criterion and its justification, human self-descriptions, and this letter receive the same treatment.

The reading procedure and worked examples:
